\abstract{Leave-one-facility-out (LOFO) cross-validation with bootstrap resampling is widely used to ask whether a data-driven scaling law generalizes across experimental sources. Two companion manuscripts \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2} put it to a 114-point windage dataset drawn from four facilities. A good in-sample fit collapsed. Whether a verdict of that kind can be trusted at such a scale is not settleable from the dataset itself, so we study the protocol against known synthetic ground truth: three passes, 18,000 scenarios. The conventional rule, LOFO R\textsuperscript{2}$>0$, copes with parameter drift and heavy-tailed noise. Against two realistic failure modes it is nearly blind, flagging 9.4\% of nonlinear-misspecified and 42.7\% of clustered confounding, and for the former it worsens as facilities are added. A facility-label permutation test, computed from the same fits, recovers most of that loss. Its validity rests on an assumption the first two passes could not expose, since they hand every facility identical covariate support: the permutation null needs the sources to overlap. Once operating windows separate, the naive test reaches 17.5\% false alarms against a nominal 5\%. Permuting labels only within covariate strata brings calibration back across the binned range, at little cost in power. We then calibrate what counts as enough overlap. The worst fold must share at least one observation with the rest; below that, power drops to 60.7\% and the plain threshold rule is safer. At one or two shared observations calibration is only approximately nominal. The companion corpus clears the bar by a single point.}

\end{Frontmatter}

\section*{Impact Statement}
\noindent Scientists increasingly audit data-driven patterns by training on some experimental sources and testing on the ones held back. The aim is to tell a transferable law from an artifact of one facility. We put that procedure to known synthetic ground truth. The usual pass/fail rule handles noise and drift, yet it is nearly blind to facility-specific nonlinearity and to structure only some sources share. A permutation-based rule repairs this, but only where the sources cover comparable operating ranges. As they separate it raises false alarms roughly threefold, and under full separation no such rule works at all. Anyone pooling data across instruments or sites should measure how far their sources overlap before trusting a verdict. We define that measurement and the resulting decision rule.

\section{Introduction}

A growing family of methods audits data-driven discovery by asking a second question. Not only does this model fit the data, but does the structure it found survive when a whole data source is withheld? The worry is concrete. Pool data from several experimental facilities, fit a regression, and a high in-sample R\textsuperscript{2} may come entirely from absorbing facility-specific offsets, with the fitted exponents corresponding to nothing transferable. Leave-one-group-out cross-validation, often paired with bootstrap resampling to obtain confidence intervals on the surviving structure, is a standard tool for detecting this failure mode \citep{kohavi1995,efron1979}.

The first two manuscripts in this series \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2} apply this audit protocol to windage (aerodynamic friction) in rotor-stator disc cavities, a problem with a long history of facility-specific correlations that do not transfer well \citep{bayley1969,daily1960}. The second companion manuscript \citep{gilruiz2026paper2} assembles 114 points from four independent facilities \citep{guo2024,vrancik1968,liu2024,zheng2024} and fits them with a four-predictor power law. In sample the fit is high; under leave-one-facility-out cross-validation it collapses, with a pooled LOFO R\textsuperscript{2} of $-0.885$. Of its coefficients, only the Reynolds-number exponent survives bootstrap resampling across facilities. A subsequent sweep of twenty-one further attempts, sixteen of them learning methods --- LLM-guided physical-law discovery, a hierarchical Bayesian neural network with a physics-informed prior, Gaussian processes and a deep-kernel variant, a set transformer, domain-adversarial networks, model-agnostic meta-learning, genetic symbolic regression and a physics-informed network, among others --- was run on the same 114 points. Every one returns a negative pooled LOFO R\textsuperscript{2}, and ten of them score \emph{worse} than the four-predictor linear fit they were meant to improve on. That study also quantifies why: the largest canonical correlation between its predictor block and the facility indicators is 0.996, so one predictor direction is very nearly a relabelling of facility identity, while the residual Reynolds-weighted direction is almost unconfounded (0.005).

Informative as it is, that negative result leaves a methodological question open which one real dataset cannot close. With four facilities, the smallest holding eight points, is the LOFO+bootstrap protocol itself trustworthy at that scale? A negative verdict means something only if the protocol reliably separates genuine non-generalization from a false alarm driven by thin data, one where the law is in fact universal and the protocol says otherwise. Settling that needs known ground truth, and only synthetic data supplies it.

This paper studies the audit protocol itself, and leaves the real windage dataset alone; the second companion manuscript has already treated it exhaustively. Under what combinations of domain count $K$, points per domain $n$, noise level, and type and strength of confounding does a LOFO+bootstrap protocol correctly tell a universal law from a facility-specific artifact? We generate thousands of synthetic multi-facility datasets whose ground truth is declared in advance rather than inferred. On each we run the same class of audit protocol the companion manuscripts use, implementing and reporting both LOFO and facility-level bootstrap. We then train a meta-model over the resulting scenarios to predict protocol reliability, using statistics a practitioner could compute from their own data together with the protocol's own observed verdict, never the generator parameters that fixed the ground truth. We then evaluate that meta-model using the real windage case's own \emph{observed} LOFO verdict as an external check, citing only figures already reported in the companion manuscript, without re-analyzing its raw data.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A synthetic benchmark (4,000 scenarios under Gaussian parameter drift, plus a second pass of 8,000 scenarios spanning four confounding mechanisms crossed with equal/unequal facility sizes) for evaluating cross-domain scaling-law audit protocols, with both LOFO cross-validation and facility-level bootstrap implemented, matching the companion studies' own methodology.
  \item A quantified characterization of when the conventional LOFO$>0$ threshold rule succeeds and fails: robust to parameter drift and heavy noise, but with a severe blind spot against nonlinear misspecification and against clustered/partial universality that, for the nonlinear mechanism, does not shrink with more data or more facilities.
  \item A facility-label permutation test computed from the same LOFO fits, shown to be a substantially more reliable declaration rule than the plain threshold across all four confounding mechanisms, at no additional data-collection cost.
  \item A third pass (6,000 scenarios) that adds covariate-support overlap between facilities as a design dimension, showing that the permutation test's validity is conditional on it: the naive test is anti-conservative once operating windows separate, a stratified variant restores calibration without losing power, and near-total separation is an identifiability limit for any label-permutation scheme. This yields a decision rule keyed to an observable statistic, which we also evaluate on the companion corpus.
  \item A meta-model that predicts the threshold rule's own verdict reliability from statistics computable from real data and the protocol's own observed output. It deliberately excludes the unobservable ground-truth label and the generator's latent noise and heterogeneity settings, which earlier drafts of this study mistakenly included (see Limitations). We apply it to the real windage case's observed verdict as an external check, with an explicit accounting of why part of that check's near-certainty is tautological and not case-specific.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Domain generalization and cross-facility audit.}
The LOFO-style protocol used here and in the companion manuscripts \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2} is a special case of leave-one-group-out validation for detecting group-specific confounding. Causal transportability \citep{pearl2011transportability} gives a formal framework for reasoning about which mechanisms carry across domains, though it has seen little application to engineering scaling-law discovery. Hierarchical Bayesian and multi-group Gaussian process models \citep{li2025multigroup} are built to separate shared from group-specific structure when the groups are few and heterogeneous. The second companion manuscript applied exactly that class of model to the real windage data \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}. No generalizing structure came out. Hence the present question of when such methods can be expected to work at all. Validating confounding-detection and cross-validation methods against known synthetic ground truth is established practice in other fields, notably observational epidemiology and biostatistics \citep{varga2023scoping}; in a literature search we did not identify a comparable simulation study for the specific combination of leave-one-facility-out cross-validation and bootstrap used for scaling-law discovery in the physical sciences and engineering. That search combined terms for the validation scheme (\emph{leave-one-group-out}, \emph{leave-one-site-out}, \emph{leave-one-facility-out}, \emph{grouped cross-validation}) with terms for the target (\emph{scaling law}, \emph{empirical correlation}, \emph{symbolic regression}, \emph{domain confounding}) across Google Scholar, Scopus and arXiv, in August 2026, and followed citations from the reviews we did find; we report it as non-exhaustive because it was not a registered systematic review.

\paragraph{Machine learning for scaling-law and equation discovery.}
Transformer-based symbolic regression has advanced quickly \citep{biggio2021nesymres,kamienny2022,tpsr2023}: pretrain on large synthetic corpora of equations, then run in inference or fine-tuning mode on a small real dataset. Parallel symbolic enumeration \citep{pse2026} shows GPU-scale search for physical laws working directly on limited experimental data. LLM-guided hypothesis generation \citep{hu2024physagent} proposes candidate functional forms from domain knowledge instead of searching blind, although benchmarks built to test whether such systems discover structure or recall memorized forms \citep{shojaee2025llmsrbench} show the question is still open. None of these methods asks, by design, whether the structure it discovered would survive a cross-domain audit. They optimize fit. Transferability is what the LOFO+bootstrap protocol targets, and what this paper stress-tests.

\paragraph{Deep learning under small-sample tabular regimes.}
Grinsztajn et al.\ \citep{grinsztajn2022} find tree-based models still ahead of deep learning on tabular data even at roughly 10,000 rows. The real windage dataset has 114 rows across four facilities. Training a deep architecture from scratch there is unlikely to be a credible improvement without stronger priors or outside data, which is why we apply machine learning to a large \emph{synthetic} scenario space instead of to the small real dataset directly.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Synthetic scenario generator}

Each scenario simulates $K$ facilities, each contributing $n_k$ points of a log-linear scaling law,
\begin{equation}
\log C_p = \beta_0^{(k)} + a^{(k)} \log Re + b^{(k)} \log \Pi_g + \varepsilon,
\qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
matching the functional form of the winning model in the second companion manuscript \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}. $Re$ and $\Pi_g$ are drawn uniformly in log-space over ranges representative of the real dataset. Each scenario is generated as either \emph{universal} or \emph{confounded}, and which one is fixed in advance by the generator. In a universal scenario $\beta_0^{(k)}, a^{(k)}, b^{(k)}$ are identical across all $K$ facilities, and the fitted log-linear model is correctly specified. A confounded scenario is one where the fitted model is not the true generating process, because the linear coefficients genuinely differ by facility or, under nonlinear misspecification, because an unmodeled term does. Four mechanisms:

\begin{description}
  \item[Gaussian parameter drift] $(\beta_0^{(k)}, a^{(k)}, b^{(k)})$ drawn independently per facility from a Normal distribution centered on the true values, spread controlled by heterogeneity $h \in [0,3]$.
  \item[Parameter drift with outlier contamination] the \emph{universal} arm uses the true shared law with no parameter drift; the \emph{confounded} arm uses the same Gaussian parameter drift as above. In both arms, additionally, 15\% of points per facility are drawn from a heavy-noise contamination population ($5\sigma$) -- testing whether the protocol confuses noise with non-generalization, independently of whether parameter drift is also present.
  \item[Nonlinear misspecification] the linear coefficients $(\beta_0, a, b)$ are truly universal, but each facility adds an independent quadratic term in $\log Re$ (scaled by $h$) that the fitted log-linear model cannot represent.
  \item[Clustered] facilities are assigned to one of two latent clusters; the law is identical within a cluster and differs between clusters by $h$, modeling \emph{partial} universality instead of all-or-nothing.
\end{description}

Facility sizes are either equal or drawn log-normally around $n$ (\texttt{n\_mode}=\texttt{unequal}), echoing the real dataset's dispersion of 45/41/20/8 points. The design parameters $K\in[2,20]$, $n\in[5,100]$, $\sigma\in[0.05,0.6]$ and $h\in[0,3]$ are sampled uniformly. A first pass of 4,000 scenarios used only Gaussian parameter drift; a second, independent pass of 8,000 scenarios (2,000 per mechanism) covers all four confounding types crossed with both facility-size modes. In both, every facility draws its covariates from the same range. The facilities therefore always share complete covariate support. A third independent pass of 6,000 scenarios relaxes exactly that (Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}). The three passes are analyzed separately throughout (Sections~\ref{sec:results-v1}, \ref{sec:results-v2} and \ref{sec:results-v3}) and are not pooled into a single meta-model.

\subsection{Audit protocol: LOFO and facility-level bootstrap}

For each scenario we fit a single pooled ordinary-least-squares model in log space on all points, ignoring facility identity, then perform leave-one-facility-out cross-validation: for each held-out facility $k$ we refit on the remaining $K-1$ facilities, obtaining $\hat\beta_{-k}$, and predict the held-out points. Pooling residuals across all folds gives
\begin{equation}
R^{2}_{\mathrm{LOFO}}
= 1-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{F}_k}\bigl(y_i-x_i^{\top}\hat\beta_{-k}\bigr)^{2}}
        {\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(y_i-\bar{y}\bigr)^{2}},
\label{eq:lofo}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_k$ indexes the points of facility $k$, $y_i=\log C_{p,i}$ and $\bar y$ is the pooled mean. This is the metric the companion manuscripts use \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2}. The protocol declares the law universal if $R^{2}_{\mathrm{LOFO}}>0$; this threshold means the pooled model out-predicts a constant (the pooled mean), the weakest non-trivial bar for generalization. Our reason for adopting it is compatibility with the companion manuscripts' own criterion, which enables direct comparison with their reported numbers; it is not the most discriminating choice available. Section~\ref{sec:results-v1} shows a stricter threshold would in fact detect confounding more reliably, at negligible cost to detecting universality.

Separately, we implement facility-level bootstrap: $B=200$ resamples of the $K$ facilities with replacement, refitting the pooled model on each resample and computing a percentile 95\% confidence interval for the Reynolds-like exponent $a$. We report whether this interval excludes zero (the coefficient ``survives'' bootstrap, the same language and criterion the companion manuscripts use) as a second, complementary diagnostic to the LOFO verdict, which we keep separate from it instead of folding both into a single binary label. A scenario is scored \emph{correct} (for the LOFO verdict) if the declared universality matches ground truth.

\paragraph{Facility-label permutation test.} As an alternative declaration rule computed from the same pooled fit at no extra data-collection cost, we additionally implement a facility-label permutation test: the observed $R^{2}_{\mathrm{LOFO}}$ is compared against its own null distribution, obtained by randomly reassigning facility labels to points and recomputing \eqref{eq:lofo} on each shuffle, closed-form via normal-equation downdating, without refitting from scratch. Writing $g$ for the vector of facility labels and $g^{(b)}$ for its $b$-th permutation,
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; \frac{1+\bigl|\{\,b\le B:\;R^{2}_{\mathrm{LOFO}}(g^{(b)})\le R^{2}_{\mathrm{LOFO}}(g)\,\}\bigr|}{1+B},
\qquad B=99,
\label{eq:perm}
\end{equation}
and the protocol declares the law confounded when $p\le\alpha=0.05$, universal otherwise. We use the one-sided form because confounding can only make the held-out fit worse than chance relabelling, never better. And where the plain threshold rule calibrates against a fixed value of zero, this test calibrates against the scenario's own noise level. Section~\ref{sec:results-v1} reports it head-to-head against the threshold rule.

\paragraph{Covariate-support overlap, and a stratified permutation variant.} The permutation null above assumes points are exchangeable across facility labels. This assumption is substantive rather than technical. It holds only if the facilities cover comparable regions of covariate space; when they do not, leave-one-facility-out becomes an \emph{extrapolation} task while the permuted pseudo-folds remain \emph{interpolation}. The observed LOFO R\textsuperscript{2} can then fall in the lower tail of the permutation null even under a genuinely universal law (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). Both passes above draw every facility's covariates from the same uniform range, i.e.\ they sit at complete overlap and cannot detect this failure mode.

\centering

\caption{Why separated support invalidates the permutation null. \emph{Top}: when the facilities share covariate range, predicting a held-out facility (red) and predicting a permuted pseudo-fold are statistically the same problem, so the null matches the observed statistic. \emph{Bottom}: when each facility occupies its own window, leave-one-facility-out becomes extrapolation, while a permuted pseudo-fold still draws points from the whole range and remains interpolation. The observed LOFO R\textsuperscript{2} is then compared against a null generated by an easier task, and a universal law can be rejected on that mismatch alone}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure} A third pass (Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}) therefore adds an overlap parameter controlling each facility's $\log Re$ window, interpolating between complete sharing of the range and a disjoint tiling of it, and evaluates two further quantities:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two observable overlap statistics}, both computable from a design matrix alone and so available to a practitioner before any rule is chosen. Write $I_k=[\min_{i\in\mathcal{F}_k}\log Re_i,\ \max_{i\in\mathcal{F}_k}\log Re_i]$ for facility $k$'s covariate range and $I_{-k}$ for the range spanned by all the others. The \emph{mean pairwise overlap} and the \emph{minimum fold overlap} are
\begin{equation}
\bar{o}=\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\!\!\sum_{k<l}\frac{|I_k\cap I_l|}{|I_k\cup I_l|},
\qquad
o_{\min}=\min_{k}\frac{|I_k\cap I_{-k}|}{|I_k\cup I_{-k}|}.
\label{eq:overlap}
\end{equation}
Across the third pass $\bar{o}$ tracks the latent design parameter closely (Pearson $r=0.995$). A third statistic replaces the range comparison by a point count, since \eqref{eq:overlap} can be lifted off zero by a single boundary observation. Writing $m_{\min}$ for the smallest number of held-out points falling inside the others' range, and $\rho_{\min}$ for the same quantity as a fraction,
\begin{equation}
m_{\min}=\min_{k}\;\bigl|\{\,i\in\mathcal{F}_k:\ \log Re_i\in I_{-k}\,\}\bigr|,
\qquad
\rho_{\min}=\min_{k}\;\frac{\bigl|\{\,i\in\mathcal{F}_k:\ \log Re_i\in I_{-k}\,\}\bigr|}{|\mathcal{F}_k|}.
\label{eq:points}
\end{equation} The decision rule of Section~\ref{sec:results-v3} keys on the last two, because a permutation test needs admissible label swaps in \emph{every} fold: one separated facility removes them from its own, and a range comparison can be lifted off zero by a single boundary observation without any usable support behind it. Results are binned on the range-based minimum, with the point-based statistic used as the check that catches that failure. The mean is reported alongside only to show how far the three can diverge on a real corpus.
  \item \textbf{A stratified permutation variant}: identical to \eqref{eq:perm}, except that permutations are drawn from
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{strat}}=\bigl\{\,\pi:\;s(\pi(i))=s(i)\ \ \text{for all } i\,\bigr\},
\label{eq:strat}
\end{equation}
where $s(i)$ is the equal-count stratum of $\log Re_i$ ($K$ strata). Labels are then exchanged only between points at comparable operating conditions, so the null holds the covariate--facility association fixed and tests only for a coefficient difference beyond what design separation alone produces.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Meta-model}

For each pass we train a gradient-boosting classifier to predict LOFO-verdict correctness. Its inputs are quantities a practitioner running this protocol could actually compute on their own data: the facility count $K$, the mean facility size $n$, the protocol's own \emph{observed} verdict (\texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}), and two proxies taken from the pooled and per-facility fits. Those are \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat}, the residual standard deviation of the single pooled fit, and \texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat}, the standard deviation across facilities of each facility's \emph{own} individually-fit Reynolds-like coefficient. Neither proxy requires knowing whether the underlying law is truly universal. Two exclusions need stating outright, since external review caught both (see Limitations). One is the ground-truth label, unobservable in practice and tautologically tied to the correctness target. Also excluded are the generator's latent \texttt{sigma} and \texttt{heterogeneity} settings: an earlier draft used them directly, even though they are generator parameters and not quantities anyone could compute from real data. For the second pass we train two separate models on the same observable feature set: an \emph{observable-only} model matching the first pass exactly, and a second \emph{oracle} model additionally given the confounding mechanism as an input. Confound type is not observable in practice. An earlier draft folded it into a single pooled accuracy figure and called the whole result observable-only, which was imprecise; that is corrected here (see Limitations). We report the oracle model separately, as a what-if upper bound and not as a claim about achievable performance. A Gaussian process classifier is trained on a subsample of the first pass as a probabilistic cross-check. Permutation importance quantifies which inputs drive predicted reliability in the observable-only models.

\subsection{External check: the real windage case's observed verdict}

We evaluate the trained meta-model at the real windage case's design point ($K=4$, mean $n\approx28.5$), using \texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}=\textsc{False} --- the \emph{observed} fact from the second companion manuscript's reported LOFO result (pooled R\textsuperscript{2}=$-0.885<0$) \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}, not an assumption about the unknown ground truth. Computing \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat} and \texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat} for the real case would require re-analyzing the companion manuscript's raw data, which this paper sets aside (see Introduction); we instead sweep both over a plausible range spanning this study's own training distribution and report how the predicted reliability varies (Section~\ref{sec:anchor}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results-v1}

\subsection{Overall protocol reliability (first pass, Gaussian drift)}

The first pass runs 4,000 Gaussian-drift scenarios, 2,013 of them truly universal and 1,987 truly confounded. Overall, the LOFO$>0$ threshold verdict is correct 90.1\% of the time. The split by ground truth is uneven: 100.0\% when the law is universal, 80.0\% when it is not. The facility-level bootstrap's Reynolds-exponent confidence interval excludes zero (the coefficient ``survives'') 100.0\% of the time when truly universal and 71.8\% of the time when truly confounded under this mechanism. That is close to the threshold verdict itself here, if slightly less reliable, which fits bootstrap and LOFO testing related but not identical things: coefficient stability against held-out predictive fit.

\paragraph{The permutation-test rule is more reliable at no extra data-collection cost.} The facility-label permutation test (Method) computed from the same pooled fits raises overall accuracy to 96.5\%: 94.2\% when truly universal (a small increase in false alarms relative to the threshold rule's 100.0\%) and 98.7\% when truly confounded (a large gain over the threshold rule's 80.0\%). This trade is real. A handful of genuinely universal scenarios receive false ``confounded'' calls; in exchange, missed detections fall by a much larger margin. At the $\alpha=0.05$ significance level used throughout, the net effect strongly favors the permutation rule, and Section~\ref{sec:results-v2} shows the gain is largest precisely where the threshold rule is weakest.

\paragraph{Part of the headline 80.0\% figure is a labeling artifact, not a protocol failure.} Table~\ref{tab:h-band} breaks the threshold rule's ``when confounded'' accuracy down by the drift strength $h$ that actually generated each scenario. At $h\in(0,0.1]$, where the confounded arm is by construction numerically close to indistinguishable from the universal arm, threshold-rule accuracy is exactly 0.0\%: the protocol is being scored wrong for correctly failing to detect confounding that is, at that drift strength, not really there to detect. Accuracy rises sharply and near-monotonically with $h$, reaching $>95\%$ by $h>1.0$. The pooled 80.0\% headline figure is therefore a mixture of genuine protocol failures (mid-range $h$) and this definitional effect (near-zero $h$); it should not be read as ``the protocol misses 20\% of real confounding'' without this caveat. The permutation rule is far less sensitive to this effect: even at $h\in(0,0.1]$ it reaches 75.4\% accuracy, rising to $>99\%$ by $h>0.25$ -- consistent with a test that measures against the scenario's own noise level instead of a fixed threshold (Method).

\paragraph{Sensitivity of the LOFO$>0$ threshold.} Table~\ref{tab:threshold} recomputes accuracy at alternative thresholds on the same scenarios. Accuracy when truly confounded climbs steadily, from 14.1\% at $-0.5$ to 92.7\% at $+0.5$, while accuracy when truly universal holds at exactly 100\% across the range tested. Threshold$=0$ is therefore conservative, though in one specific sense only: it protects against declaring confounding on data that is really universal. As a bar for declaring universality it is the weakest available, and it is not the setting that maximizes detection. Within the range tested a stricter threshold would catch genuine confounding more often at no measured cost to detecting genuine universality. We keep threshold$=0$ for direct comparability with the companion manuscripts' own reported numbers rather than tuning it for this study's own benefit -- but Section~\ref{sec:perm-vs-threshold} shows a permutation rule improves on this entire tradeoff curve, without a hand-picked threshold, wherever the facilities share enough covariate support for one to be valid (Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}).

\subsection{Confounding mechanism determines reliability more than sample size (second pass)}
\label{sec:results-v2}
\label{sec:perm-vs-threshold}

Table~\ref{tab:by-type} and Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-by-type} report LOFO-verdict accuracy separately for ground-truth-universal and ground-truth-confounded scenarios, by mechanism, from the independent second pass (2,000 scenarios per mechanism), for both the threshold rule and the permutation rule (Method). Under the threshold rule, robustness to Gaussian parameter drift (82.3\%) and to outlier contamination (78.2\%) is comparable. Nonlinear misspecification is where it fails worst. Only 9.4\% of genuinely misspecified scenarios are flagged as non-generalizing, on a 95\% interval of [7.8, 11.4]\%. The other 90.6\% of the time, a facility-specific nonlinear effect the linear model cannot represent gets read as evidence of a universal law. Clustered, partial universality is also poorly detected by the threshold rule (42.7\%, 95\% CI [39.7,45.7]\%): it mistakes ``some facilities share structure'' for ``all facilities share structure'' 57.3\% of the time.

\paragraph{The permutation rule closes most of this gap.} The same second-pass scenarios, scored instead by the facility-label permutation test (Method), tell a markedly different story: 99.0\%, 97.6\%, 89.4\%, and 96.6\% detection when truly confounded under Gaussian drift, outlier contamination, nonlinear misspecification, and clustered confounding respectively --- turning the threshold rule's worst cases (nonlinear misspecification, clustered) into its best. Pooled over all 8,000 second-pass scenarios, the permutation rule reaches 95.1\% overall accuracy (per-mechanism overall accuracy 92.1--96.9\%; full breakdown in \texttt{results/synth\_scenarios\_v2\_summary.json}), against 76.1\% pooled (53.8--91.2\% per mechanism) for the threshold rule. Replacing the plain LOFO$>0$ threshold with a facility-label permutation test therefore costs essentially nothing in data collection, and it brings all four mechanisms tested under this generator design to comparable reliability. Section~\ref{sec:results-v3} sets out when that substitution is actually available.

\paragraph{Detection power under the threshold rule does not improve with more data.} For nonlinear misspecification specifically, threshold-rule detection accuracy on genuinely confounded scenarios does not rise with sample size and \emph{falls} with facility count. Binning $n$ at edges 15/30/50/75/100 points per facility, accuracy is 13.7\% at $n\leq15$ down to 7.3\% at $n\in(76,100]$; binning $K$ at edges 3/6/10/15/20 facilities, accuracy is 35.9\% at $K\in[2,3]$ down to 0.7\% at $K\in(15,20]$. The permutation rule shows the opposite pattern on the same scenarios, rising from 60.4\% ($n\leq15$) to 91.6--95.3\% ($n\geq16$) and from 75.6\% ($K$=2--3) to 93.6\% ($K$=16--20). We do not derive a closed-form explanation for why the threshold rule's power degrades here instead of merely staying low (a natural next step, left to future work); empirically, this is further evidence against ``collect more facilities'' as a fix for the threshold rule's blind spot, and further support for switching to the permutation rule instead.

For nonlinear misspecification the facility-level bootstrap tells a different story. Its Reynolds-exponent interval excludes zero, and so ``survives'', 98.8\% of the time even when the scenario is truly confounded, against 86.8\% for clustered confounding and 70.9--71.0\% for the other two mechanisms (raw figures in \texttt{results/synth\_scenarios\_v2\_summary.json}). The two diagnostics are not in contradiction. Nor is this a case where LOFO does its job well. Under nonlinear misspecification the true linear coefficients \emph{are} universal by construction, only the unmodeled quadratic term varying by facility, so the bootstrap is right to call the linear structure stable. What the bootstrap cannot see is the unmodeled term, and that term does hurt held-out prediction. The damage simply escapes the LOFO R\textsuperscript{2}$>0$ threshold 90.6\% of the time, because the pooled prediction error it causes is usually too small to drive pooled LOFO R\textsuperscript{2} below zero. LOFO and bootstrap answer different questions, predictive generalization against coefficient stability, and this mechanism is where the two diagnostics can legitimately part company. Here LOFO is the less reliable of them.

\subsection{Meta-model: predicting verdict reliability from observable information}

What this meta-model predicts is the \emph{threshold rule's} own verdict correctness, and not the permutation rule's. Sections~\ref{sec:results-v1}--\ref{sec:perm-vs-threshold} already establish that the permutation rule is preferable in absolute terms. What this model quantifies is how far a threshold-rule verdict should be trusted, which matters to readers of the companion manuscripts, since that is the rule they used. One caveat governs the numbers below. The target \texttt{correct} is itself imbalanced: the threshold rule is right 90.1\% of the time on the first pass and 76.1\% on the second. Accuracy is therefore reported against a same-target majority-class baseline, always predicting the more common value of \texttt{correct} within the held-out split. That baseline is 76.8\% on the second pass, close to but not identical with the 76.1\% pooled accuracy quoted earlier, which is computed over all 8,000 scenarios instead of the 25\% test split. Neither 50\% nor the unrelated ground-truth-label base rate would serve. An earlier draft omitted this.

On the first pass the gradient-boosting meta-model reaches 96.6\% test accuracy (AUC 0.952) against a 90.1\% same-target baseline. Its inputs are fully observable (see Method): $K$, $n$, \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat}, \texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat} and the protocol's own output. A Gaussian process cross-check reaches 95.9\% accuracy at AUC 0.825. The lower AUC on similar hard-label accuracy points to less well-calibrated probabilities. On the second pass, the same observable-only feature set (plus \texttt{n\_mode\_unequal}) reaches a lower 85.5\% (AUC 0.893) against a 76.8\% baseline, a modest but real +8.7-point gain and smaller than the raw accuracy figure alone would suggest; per-mechanism accuracy ranges from 77.4\% (nonlinear-misspecified) to 97.0\% (Gaussian drift). A separate \emph{oracle} variant reaches 91.8\% (AUC 0.935), fifteen points over baseline. It is additionally told the confounding mechanism, which no practitioner knows with certainty, so we report it as a what-if upper bound on how much knowing the mechanism could help. It is not an observable-only result. Permutation importance for the observable-only model (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}) shows the protocol's own observed verdict and \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat} jointly dominate; \texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat} contributes moderately, raw sample size ($K$, $n$) comparatively little.

\subsection{Covariate-support overlap decides which rule is valid (third pass)}
\label{sec:results-v3}

The permutation rule's advantage in Sections~\ref{sec:results-v1}--\ref{sec:results-v2} was established on scenarios where all facilities share complete covariate support. Table~\ref{tab:overlap} and Figures~\ref{fig:overlap} and \ref{fig:surface} report the third pass (6,000 scenarios, overlap sampled uniformly), scoring all three declaration rules on the universal arm (where any rejection is a false alarm) and the confounded arm (where any rejection is a correct detection).

\paragraph{The binding statistic is the worst fold, not the average pair.} Our index for these results is the \emph{minimum} over LOFO folds, in place of the mean over facility pairs. The distinction matters, because LOFO predicts one held-out facility at a time. A stratified permutation has admissible label swaps for a fold only when the held-out facility shares covariate range with the rest, so one separated facility disables the test for its own fold, however well the remaining facilities agree among themselves. A corpus can thus post a comfortable mean and a marginal minimum. The companion corpus below is that case. Both statistics come from the design matrix alone; we key on the minimum because it binds.

Only where the folds overlap well is the naive permutation test calibrated. As the operating windows separate, its false-alarm rate climbs from 5.6--6.8\% in the top two bins, about the nominal $\alpha=0.05$ and the regime both earlier passes occupy, to 17.5\% in the lowest, roughly \emph{threefold} anti-conservative. The mechanism is the one the Method anticipates. With separated windows, predicting a held-out facility means extrapolating, while the permuted pseudo-folds keep interpolating, so a universal law looks anomalously bad against its own null. By construction the threshold rule cannot do this, and it produced no false alarm at any overlap level in this pass, which is what makes it a safe fallback.

Stratifying the permutation repairs this, and cheaply. With swaps confined to within a stratum, the false-alarm rate stays at 4.4--6.8\% across the whole range and every bin's 95\% interval in Table~\ref{tab:overlap} covers the nominal 0.05. Power barely suffers: 97.6\% in the lowest bin and 100\% elsewhere, against 81.8--88.5\% for the threshold rule. The stratified test is thus the best of the three on both criteria at once; wherever it is admissible, we recommend it.

\paragraph{Where the advantage ends.} The lowest bin of Table~\ref{tab:overlap} still shows the stratified test working, so the boundary lies inside it. Two further batches locate it (\texttt{results/overlap\_fine\_sweep.json}, \texttt{results/overlap\_transition.json}). The transition is abrupt, and it sits at zero. Where every fold retains \emph{some} positive overlap the stratified test keeps full power: 98.4\% at minimum fold overlap 0.003--0.006, 98.5\% at 0.006--0.010, 100.0\% at 0.010--0.020. Once one fold is exactly disjoint, power falls to 56.2\% ($n{=}162$; \texttt{lowest\_bin\_split\_at\_exact\_zero} in \texttt{results/overlap\_fine\_sweep.json}). That fold admits no label swap, so it contributes nothing and the remaining folds carry the test alone. When \emph{all} facilities are mutually disjoint the test goes inert. A dedicated batch of 150 scenarios per arm at overlap $=0$ (\texttt{degenerate\_corner\_overlap\_zero} in \texttt{results/synth\_scenarios\_v3\_summary.json}) returns 0.0\% false alarms and 0.0\% power. Facility identity has become a deterministic function of the covariates, every stratum holds a single facility, and nothing can be rejected. The naive test in that same corner reaches 20.0\% false alarms; the threshold rule holds 0.0\% at 85.3\% power. This inertia reflects the problem itself rather than any defect of the estimator: under perfect separation, no argument that permutes facility labels can tell ``the facilities follow different laws'' apart from ``the facilities were run at different conditions''.

\paragraph{How much shared support is enough.} Because the boundary sits at exactly zero, the range statistic is fragile there: one observation lifts it off zero. Whether that matters is an empirical question. Table~\ref{tab:points} bins scenarios by how many points of the held-out facility actually fall inside the range the others span, on the fold where that count is smallest, at 140 scenarios per arm per bin. One point is enough. Power is 60.7\% with no point shared and 100.0\% (95\% CI [0.973, 1.000]) from one point onwards, flat across every larger bin. Calibration over the same range is roughly nominal but noisy, between 2.9\% and 12.1\%, with two of six intervals excluding 0.05 and no monotone trend. We read that as sampling noise around a mildly anti-conservative test, and would not call it structure.

So the criterion is whether the worst fold shares \emph{any} support at all, not how much, which is weaker than the range statistics suggest. The two disagree only in the narrow band where the count is one or two (Limitations).

\paragraph{Where the companion corpus falls.} We computed all three statistics on the companion manuscripts' 114-point windage corpus, working from the aggregate design statistics in Appendix~\ref{app:companion} and not from any re-analysis of its windage relationship (\texttt{results/companion\_overlap.json}). Its mean pairwise overlap in $\log Re$ is 0.317. Its minimum fold overlap is 0.008, just above the transition located above. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} shows why that margin is thin. On the fold holding out the largest facility, the shared region spans $\log_{10} Re \in [6.796, 6.819]$ and holds exactly one of that facility's 45 points, alongside one of the other 69. By fold the counts run 1 of 45, 20 of 20, 31 of 41 and 8 of 8.

By Table~\ref{tab:points}, one point suffices. The stratified permutation test is admissible on this corpus, then, at the narrowest margin the criterion allows. From a range overlap of 0.008 and a single shared point the natural inference runs the other way, that no permutation is possible. The calibration does not bear it out.

The companion study reaches the same diagnosis from a different direction, and the two measurements are independent. Its identifiability index reports a canonical correlation of 0.996 between the predictor block and the facility indicators \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}: one predictor direction is very nearly a relabelling of facility identity. That is the condition this section identifies as the one under which label-permutation arguments fail, arrived at there by canonical correlation on the fitted design and here by counting shared observations per fold. Both say the corpus sits at the edge of what any facility-label permutation can resolve.

Two qualifications keep this from being a clean endorsement. For one, the corpus sits where the test's calibration is least certain: 7.1\% false alarms at one shared point, on an interval running to 12.6\%, so a rejection there is suggestive rather than decisive. For another, the margin rests on a single observation. Had that one Guo point fallen marginally higher, the fold would have no shared support and the test would shed roughly 39 points of power. The companion studies used the threshold rule, and its verdict stands regardless. A reanalysis with the stratified rule would be admissible, and useful, provided the calibration caveat travels with it.

\subsection{External check: the real windage case's observed verdict}
\label{sec:anchor}

We evaluate at the real windage case's design point, $K{=}4$ and $n\approx28.5$, using its \emph{observed} LOFO verdict: \texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}=\textsc{False}, from the second companion manuscript's pooled LOFO R\textsuperscript{2}=$-0.885$ \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}. Over a 25-point grid spanning plausible \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat}$\in[0.1,0.5]$ and \texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat}$\in[0.1,1.2]$, the gradient-boosting meta-model puts the probability that the verdict is correct at 0.997--0.999, mean 0.999, everywhere on the grid. The Gaussian process cross-check gives 0.627--0.633, mean 0.630. Markedly lower, and just as flat.

\paragraph{The GBM's near-certainty here is a property of the generator, not case-specific evidence.} We checked this directly instead of taking the flat 0.997--0.999 sweep at face value. Across all 1,590 first-pass scenarios sharing the real case's observed verdict (\texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}=\textsc{False}), regardless of $K$, $n$, or the observable proxies, \texttt{correct}=1 exactly 1.0000 of the time (1,590/1,590, \texttt{results/meta\_model\_results.json}). The reason is structural: because the universal arm of this generator carries no facility-to-facility deviation, nothing in it can produce a spurious LOFO$<0$, and a genuinely universal scenario almost never yields \texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}=\textsc{False} at all. Conditioning on that observed verdict therefore all but fixes the ground truth as confounded, whatever else is true of the scenario. The GBM has learned that regularity of the generator correctly, but about the real windage case it has learned nothing. The flat sweep above is the symptom. A probability that responded meaningfully to $K$, $n$ or the observable proxies would make the sweep vary; it does not. Throughout, the only decisive feature is \texttt{protocol\_says\_universal}, held at \textsc{False}. Of the two numbers, the GP's lower and noisier estimate is arguably the more honest: its RBF kernel cannot represent a near-deterministic relationship cleanly, so it drifts toward the surrounding class balance instead of memorizing a quirk of this generator.

The check retains some value, though far less than a 99.7--99.9\% figure suggests: it shows that this generator design almost never returns a false ``not universal'' verdict on truly universal data. Take the companion study's LOFO$<0$ result at face value and a false alarm becomes an unlikely explanation for it, \emph{under the mechanisms this generator represents}. What it does \emph{not} show is that the real case's own confounding is of a kind this protocol reliably detects. Table~\ref{tab:by-type} puts threshold-rule detection at 9.4\% for nonlinear misspecification and 42.7\% for clustered confounding, and the anchor computation cannot say which mechanism, if any, governs the real windage data (its true \texttt{obs\_sigma\_hat}/\texttt{obs\_hetero\_hat} are not computed here, by design, to avoid re-analyzing the companion manuscript's raw data). We therefore downgrade this check, relative to an earlier draft, from ``support for the companion finding'' to: a demonstration that the specific failure mode of a false-positive confounding call is implausible under this generator, alongside an explicit acknowledgment that it cannot rule out the harder-to-detect mechanisms this same paper identifies. A reader wanting a genuine external check specific to the real case's own confounding mechanism would need the raw-data re-analysis this paper's scope rules out.

\section{Discussion}

The recommendation this study supports is a two-step procedure rather than a single rule. Figure~\ref{fig:decision} states it in full. \emph{The first step is to measure how far the data sources actually overlap in covariate space}, using the minimum over leave-one-out folds of the intersection-over-union between the held-out source's range and the union of the others' (Method). Nothing but the design matrix is needed. It must be the minimum and not an average across pairs, because one separated source disables the test for its own fold however well the others agree. \emph{Second, choose the declaration rule accordingly}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Every fold shares at least one observation with the rest}: use the \emph{stratified} facility-label permutation test. The criterion counts points instead of comparing ranges, and the threshold is one (Table~\ref{tab:points}); where the count is one or two, treat a rejection as suggestive, since calibration there is only approximately nominal. It is computed from the same fits a LOFO audit already produces, requires no additional data collection, closes most of the gap between the threshold rule's best mechanism (Gaussian drift, 82.3\% detection) and its worst (nonlinear misspecification, 9.4\% detection), and unlike the naive permutation test it stays calibrated as the sources separate (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}).
  \item \textbf{Some fold shares no observation at all with the rest}: use the plain LOFO$>0$ threshold. It detects less, but it is by far the most conservative of the three: no false alarm at any overlap level in the third pass, and 4 in 3,959 universal scenarios across the second. A stratified test drops to 60.7\% power there, below the threshold rule's own 80.0\% (Table~\ref{tab:points}).
\end{itemize}

The naive permutation test is the one we would now advise against as a default, though the first two passes alone would have recommended it without qualification. It is the strongest option when supports overlap and the weakest when they do not, and the LOFO output alone does not tell a practitioner which case they are in. That the earlier passes could not see this carries a lesson of its own. A benchmark that samples one corner of the design space will confidently recommend a method that fails outside it.

Where a LOFO+bootstrap audit has already been run with the plain threshold, as in the companion manuscripts, this study's role is instead diagnostic: knowing which regime the threshold rule is in changes how much its verdict should be trusted.

A second implication cuts against the intuitive fix of collecting more facilities. Once the type and strength of confounding are accounted for, raw facility count predicts the threshold rule's reliability poorly within the range tested, and for nonlinear misspecification it does worse than that, since more facilities make the rule \emph{worse} (Section~\ref{sec:results-v2}). The practical guidance this study supports is: \emph{know, or test for, the shape of the suspected confounding before trusting a threshold-rule LOFO verdict. Or use a permutation rule, which does not require knowing it, once you have checked that the sources overlap enough for one to be valid.} A threshold-rule audit that declares ``universal'' should not be read as ruling out facility-specific structure if that structure could plausibly be nonlinear or clustered. The threshold rule is nearly blind to both; the permutation rules are not (Table~\ref{tab:by-type}). What does help is arranging that the sources share operating conditions, which can be designed for in advance. Overlap is what buys access to the more powerful rules (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}). How a corpus is assembled decides it; how many facilities it holds does not. One implication follows for the second companion manuscript \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}, which used the threshold rule. Its finding of \emph{non-generalization} sits in the regime where that rule is reliable, and Section~\ref{sec:anchor} shows the generator design here rarely returns a false ``not universal'' call on truly universal data. Confirming it more specifically is possible in principle, since the corpus does meet the admissibility criterion (Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}). It meets it by one observation, though, in the regime where calibration is least certain. Any such reanalysis would carry that caveat, and could not stand as a clean second opinion. Any future work in this series reporting a \emph{positive} generalization result from the threshold rule alone should be treated cautiously unless nonlinear and clustered alternatives have been separately ruled out, ideally by also checking the permutation-rule verdict.

\section{Limitations}

The generator uses one base functional form, the two-predictor log-linear power law that won in the second companion manuscript \citep{gilruiz2026paper2}. Other functional forms, higher-dimensional predictor sets and non-log-linear ground truths go unexplored. Each scenario is a single random draw, never repeated across seeds, so scenario-level accuracy estimates carry sampling noise the point estimates do not fully show (binomial 95\% confidence intervals are reported in Table~\ref{tab:by-type} for the headline per-mechanism figures; individual cells in finer breakdowns, e.g. $K$ bins in Figure~\ref{fig:k-hard}, are noisier still and not individually interval-estimated). The real-case anchor sweeps both observable proxies over a plausible range instead of computing their true values, by design, to avoid re-analyzing the companion manuscript's raw data. It should therefore be read as an illustrative sensitivity analysis rather than a precise recovery, and its two meta-models disagree by a wide margin (Section~\ref{sec:anchor}), two facts that should temper the weight put on it.

Three quantities are excluded from the meta-model's feature set, and a reader should know each was once included by mistake: the scenario's ground-truth label, which trivially determines the correctness target; the generator's latent noise and heterogeneity settings, not computable from real data despite an earlier claim otherwise; and the confounding mechanism, kept in the oracle variant alone and reported apart from the observable-only result. We record them because they bear on how the accuracy numbers should be read, not as narrative detail.

A bug in the clustered-mechanism generator, fixed before the results reported here, could assign every facility to the same latent cluster with probability $\approx1/2^{K-1}$ per scenario. That silently mislabeled a handful of nominally confounded clustered scenarios as truly universal, about 5\% of draws at small $K$. Every second-pass number in this manuscript postdates the fix, which is rejection sampling requiring at least two distinct clusters. We flag it so that a reader comparing against an earlier internal draft does not meet an unexplained shift in the clustered-mechanism figures.

The four confounding mechanisms are not calibrated to a common effect size: the heterogeneity parameter $h$ enters the intercept, the linear slopes, and the nonlinear-misspecification quadratic term with different fixed multipliers, so at equal $h$ the mechanisms do not necessarily perturb the fitted model by an equal amount. Part of the reported ordering of mechanisms by threshold-rule detection difficulty (Table~\ref{tab:by-type}) may therefore partly reflect this generator design choice and not a mechanism-intrinsic property; we did not attempt to re-calibrate the mechanisms to a common effect-size scale, and flag the ordering across mechanisms as correspondingly less certain than the within-mechanism findings (e.g.\ the permutation-vs-threshold comparison, Table~\ref{tab:by-type}, which compares two rules on the identical scenarios and is not affected by this issue). Relatedly, and left entirely to future work: we conjecture, without deriving or independently verifying it here, that the threshold rule's detection power for nonlinear misspecification may be structurally near-zero, not merely low, when the facility-specific nonlinear term is close to orthogonal to the fitted model's column space -- consistent with, but a stronger claim than, the empirical power curves in Section~\ref{sec:results-v2}.

The overlap analysis has boundaries of its own. It separates facilities along one covariate, $\log Re$, which is the axis on which the companion corpus actually separates. Real designs can differ along several at once, or in ways that are not simple window shifts. How the two permutation rules behave under those richer forms of separation we have not characterized. The stratified variant uses $K$ equal-count strata throughout. That is a defensible default and was never tuned; the number of strata trades control of design confounding against the supply of admissible swaps, and we did not try to optimize it. The third pass also uses only the Gaussian-drift confounding mechanism, so the overlap effect is characterized cleanly but is not crossed with the other three mechanisms, and the cutoff below which the stratified test should be abandoned falls at zero shared observations rather than being a tunable threshold, characterized here on one covariate and one confounding mechanism only. The route to that finding is worth recording, because it went wrong twice in the same direction. One version asserted the companion corpus was untestable, extrapolating from the exactly-disjoint batch to its range overlap of 0.008. The fine sweep does not support the extrapolation. A later version rescued the same conclusion by arguing that a single shared point cannot furnish usable permutations. Table~\ref{tab:points} does not support that either, and the corpus turns out to be admissible. Both inferences were plausible, both drew on measured quantities, and both were wrong. That is the case for calibrating an admissibility criterion instead of reasoning about it. Calibration in the one- and two-point bins is itself the least certain part of that table, so the criterion is best read as a floor and not as a licence. Finally, the third pass fixes facility sizes as equal and omits the bootstrap diagnostic, both of which are characterized in the earlier passes but not re-crossed with overlap here.

\section{Conclusion}

A three-pass synthetic study, with both LOFO cross-validation and facility-level bootstrap implemented, shows the following. Under the conventional LOFO$>0$ rule, a standard cross-domain audit protocol for scaling-law discovery handles simple parameter drift and heavy noise, but carries severe and quantified blind spots against nonlinear model misspecification and against clustered universality. Neither shrinks with more facilities, and the nonlinear one grows. A facility-label permutation test, computed from the same fits, closes most of this gap. That validity is conditional, on something the earlier passes could not see. The permutation null requires the facilities to share covariate support, and as their operating windows separate the naive test reaches 17.5\% false alarms against a nominal 5\%. Permuting labels only within covariate strata brings calibration back within the nominal interval across the binned range, at little cost in power, and that is the rule we recommend. As a fallback, the plain threshold rule is much the most conservative of the three: 4 false alarms across 3,959 universal second-pass scenarios, and none at all in the overlap sweep. Under near-total separation, where no label-permutation argument can separate a different law from a different design, it is the rule that takes over. Which rule applies depends on statistics computable from the practitioner's own design matrix, the minimum across leave-one-out folds rather than an average across pairs, and a point count in place of a range comparison, which we define and whose criterion we calibrate. Where the worst fold shares at least one observation, the stratified test is licensed, though calibration there is only approximately nominal; where it shares none, the test loses 39 points of power. An observable-only meta-model estimator, applied to the real windage case's own observed threshold-rule verdict \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2}, returns near-certainty. That certainty is largely tautological. Its two implementations span 0.627--0.999, and we verify directly that the higher number reflects a structural property of the generator, not case-specific evidence. What survives is a narrower claim: a false-positive confounding call is an unlikely explanation for that verdict. This is an illustrative check; it does not independently confirm that the real case's mechanism is one this protocol reliably detects. The three manuscripts together cover the audit protocol, its application to a real corpus, and the boundaries of the protocol's own reliability, including a remedy for its main weakness and the conditions under which that remedy holds.

\section{Minimum Verifiable Facts from the Companion Manuscripts}
\label{app:companion}

The motivation for this paper, and the external check in Section~\ref{sec:anchor}, rest on numbers from two unpublished companion manuscripts \citep{gilruiz2026paper1,gilruiz2026paper2}. A reader of this paper alone cannot inspect them. To limit how much of the argument sits on our own say-so, we restate the small set of facts those sections actually use, each checked against the companion manuscripts themselves rather than reproduced from memory:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The real windage dataset has $N=114$ points across four facilities, with reported sizes 45, 41, 20, and 8.
  \item The companion study's winning pooled model (log-linear, four Buckingham-$\Pi$ predictors) reaches in-sample R\textsuperscript{2}$=0.870$ but pooled leave-one-facility-out R\textsuperscript{2}$=-0.885$ in log space.
  \item Of that model's coefficients, only the Reynolds-number exponent's bootstrap confidence interval excludes zero; the companion manuscript reports this as the sole coefficient that survives facility-level bootstrap resampling.
  \item The four facilities' $\log_{10} Re$ windows are $[6.80, 7.91]$ (Guo, $n{=}45$), $[5.47, 6.82]$ (Liu, $n{=}20$), $[4.96, 6.72]$ (Vrancik, $n{=}41$) and $[5.57, 6.51]$ (Zheng, $n{=}8$), giving pairwise support overlaps of 0.009, 0.000 and 0.000 between the largest facility and each of the other three, and 0.530--0.691 among those three: a mean pairwise overlap of 0.317, but a minimum fold overlap of 0.008 (Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}).
  \item The number of each facility's points lying inside the $\log Re$ range spanned by the other three, per leave-one-facility-out fold, is 1 of 45 (Guo), 20 of 20 (Liu), 31 of 41 (Vrancik) and 8 of 8 (Zheng). The corpus spans $Re_\Omega$ from $9.06\times10^{4}$ to $8.15\times10^{7}$, a 900-fold range.
  \item The companion study's own identifiability index reports a largest canonical correlation of 0.996 between its four-predictor block and the facility indicators, with a residual Reynolds-weighted direction at 0.005.
\end{itemize}

Eight window endpoints and four counts, and that is everything on which Section~\ref{sec:results-v3}'s conclusion about this corpus rests; both overlap statistics follow from them without the dataset. The rounded windows give a mean pairwise overlap of 0.318 and a minimum fold overlap of 0.007, against 0.317 and 0.008 from the full data, the gap being the rounding printed here. \texttt{from\_published\_windows} in \texttt{code/companion\_overlap.py} performs the recomputation.

The last two items say where each facility sits in Reynolds space and how many of its points share range with the others. Both describe the corpus's design rather than the windage relationship, so reporting them does not breach the scope limit below; the first is in principle readable from the four original published sources \citep{guo2024,vrancik1968,liu2024,zheng2024}. They appear because Section~\ref{sec:results-v3} makes the choice of declaration rule depend on them, and no reader can check that choice without them. \texttt{code/companion\_overlap.py} computes them from the compiled dataset file held by the companion study's authors. We do not redistribute that file, only the aggregate statistics it yields (\texttt{results/companion\_overlap.json}). The script exits without effect when the file is absent, so every other result in this repository reproduces without it.

We do not restate, and did not re-derive, any finer-grained breakdown (e.g.\ per-facility fitted coefficients or $C_p$ values) from the companion manuscripts' raw data, since doing so would constitute the re-analysis this paper's scope deliberately excludes (Introduction). A reader who needs to audit these facts directly should request the companion manuscripts or their eventual published versions from the corresponding author.
